The history of coffee

Tea's origin legend comes with a precise year attached: 2737 BCE. Coffee's doesn't get one at all, and that gap is itself revealing: the story people tell about coffee's discovery wasn't even written down until the drink had already been circulating for centuries. This is the real chronology underneath the legend, from a goatherd's goats to the machine that made espresso possible in seconds.

The legend without a date

The most repeated origin story credits Kaldi, an Ethiopian goatherd, who supposedly noticed his goats becoming unusually energetic after eating the red cherries of a certain shrub, tried the cherries himself, and discovered coffee's effects firsthand. It's a good story, and also a late one: the earliest known written version of it doesn't appear until 1671, in a treatise by the Maronite scholar Antoine Faustus Nairon, describing something that, if it happened at all, would have happened many centuries earlier. Kaldi is a folkloric figure, not a documented historical one, and unlike Shennong's tea legend, no specific century is even attached to him with any consistency.

15th century: the first real evidence, in Yemen

The actual documented trail starts in Yemen. By the 1400s, coffee drinking is credibly attested in Sufi monasteries across Yemen and the wider Arabian Peninsula, where monks brewed it specifically to stay alert through long nights of prayer. That's coffee's equivalent of the tea page's Han tomb evidence: not a legend, but a real, dateable, functional use, arguably medicinal or devotional before it was ever social.

16th century: coffeehouses and the Ottoman world

From Yemen, coffee moved north into the Ottoman Empire, and with it came an entirely new kind of institution: the coffeehouse, a public venue built specifically around the drink rather than around food or alcohol. Istanbul's coffeehouses, some of the earliest anywhere, became centres of conversation, music, and political discussion, popular and controversial enough that Ottoman authorities periodically tried, and failed, to shut them down.

1600s: coffee reaches Europe

Venetian trade with the Ottoman world brought coffee into Europe in the early 1600s, and the coffeehouse model came with it. Venice had its own coffeehouses by around 1645; England followed by mid-century, with Oxford's first coffeehouse opening in 1650 and London's in 1652. One of those London coffeehouses, run by Edward Lloyd from 1686, became the specific meeting place where shipping merchants and insurers negotiated marine insurance deals: it eventually grew into Lloyd's of London, still one of the world's best-known insurance markets, and still carrying the name of a coffeehouse in its own history.

1699 to 1727: three thefts that ended a monopoly

For roughly two centuries, coffee cultivation itself stayed almost entirely confined to Yemen and the Arabian Peninsula, guarded closely enough that exporting a live plant, rather than roasted or boiled beans, was effectively forbidden. That monopoly broke in stages, through what amounted to a string of plant thefts. In 1699, a Dutch trader carried a coffee plant from Malabar, on India's southwest coast (itself already grown from earlier smuggled stock) to Java, establishing Dutch colonial coffee production in what is now Indonesia. In 1723, the French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu smuggled a cutting from a Dutch coffee plant onto a ship bound for Martinique, and, according to his own account, shared his limited drinking water with the seedling during a drought at sea and fought off a fellow passenger who tried to destroy it. That single plant is credited with seeding French coffee cultivation across the Caribbean. And in 1727, the Brazilian officer Francisco de Melo Palheta returned from a diplomatic mission to French Guiana with smuggled coffee seeds, planting the crop that would eventually make Brazil the largest coffee producer on earth.

1884: the machine that made espresso possible

For roughly 150 years after that, coffee spread across continents but its preparation stayed simple: ground beans, hot water, and time. The machine that changed that arrived in Turin in 1884, when the Italian inventor Angelo Moriondo patented a steam-driven apparatus that pushed pressurised hot water through a bed of coffee grounds on demand, brewing far faster than any pot-based method. Moriondo's machine won a bronze medal at that year's General Expo of Turin, and though later inventors (particularly Luigi Bezzera in the early 1900s) refined the design into what became the modern espresso machine, Moriondo is generally credited with the foundational patent: the moment coffee brewing itself became a mechanical, on-demand process rather than a matter of steeping and waiting.

1901 to 1910: instant coffee becomes a mass product

The other industrial leap happened on the powder side. Rudimentary instant-coffee concepts had existed since at least the late 18th century, but the first commercially viable method for producing a stable, soluble coffee powder is credited to the Japanese-American chemist Satori Kato, who patented his process in 1901. The step that actually turned it into a mass-market product came shortly after: around 1910, the Guatemalan-American inventor George Constant Louis Washington developed the first large-scale commercial production of instant coffee in the United States, and demand for a cheap, shelf-stable coffee that required no equipment, no grinding and no waiting only grew from there, particularly once military demand during the World Wars normalised it for an entire generation of consumers.

From ritual to commodity

Laid end to end, the story runs from an undated folk tale about a goatherd's goats, to real documented use in Yemeni monasteries by the 1400s, to a new kind of public institution built entirely around the drink, to a two-century monopoly broken by a sequence of smuggled plants and cuttings that seeded three continents' worth of coffee industries, to two industrial-era inventions, one mechanical and one chemical, that turned coffee into the fast, mass-produced commodity it is today. What started as a devotional aid for staying awake through prayer became something brewed in seconds or dissolved in hot water in an instant, without changing what's actually in the cup.
