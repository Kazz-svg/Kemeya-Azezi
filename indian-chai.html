Indian chai

"Chai" simply means "tea," a word that travelled overland from the Chinese "cha" into Hindi, Urdu, Persian, Russian and many other languages. In much of the West it has narrowed to mean one specific thing: sweet, spiced, milky black tea, what's more precisely called masala chai in India itself. This page is about that drink: how it became a daily ritual for hundreds of millions of people, what actually goes into a cup, and how it changes from one region to the next.

Not always a daily habit

Tea wasn't always central to Indian daily life. Before the 20th century, tea in much of northern India was used mainly as an occasional, medicinal drink, while everyday beverages leaned toward dairy: buttermilk, yogurt drinks and plain milk. British colonial tea production, centred on Assam from the 1830s, was built almost entirely for export to Britain, not for a domestic Indian market that barely existed yet.

How chai became India's daily drink

That changed through a deliberate, decades-long marketing effort. Starting in 1901, the British Indian Tea Association ran a campaign lasting over 40 years to build domestic demand, distributing free tea at railway stations, post offices, factories and religious festivals. The Indian Tea Market Expansion Board later sent "tea propagandists" travelling the country in vans, handing out sample cups and small "pice packets" of tea by the million. Street vendors, the chai wallahs, played their own role between the two World Wars: to stretch relatively expensive tea leaves further, they started brewing them with milk, sugar and spices, producing a fuller-tasting, more affordable cup. That improvised cost-saving habit is essentially the template for modern masala chai. By some widely cited estimates, Indians consumed more than 70% of the country's own tea output through most of the 20th century, a striking reversal from the export-only model the industry started with.

What actually goes into a cup

Classic masala chai isn't tea with spices added afterward; the tea, milk, water, sugar and spices are typically boiled together in one pot, which is a meaningfully different technique from steeping leaves and adding milk separately. The base tea is usually a CTC (crush, tear, curl) processed Assam black tea: a method invented in 1931 at the Amgoorie Tea Estate in Assam, which breaks leaves into small, dense pellets that brew fast and strong. That's precisely why it works for chai: unlike a delicate orthodox-leaf tea such as Darjeeling, CTC tea holds up to boiling and milk without disappearing under them. The spice blend (the actual "masala") varies by household and region, but commonly draws on cardamom (often the dominant note), fresh ginger, cloves, cinnamon and black pepper, with fennel or star anise sometimes added.

Regional variations

  • Mumbai's cutting chai: strong, well-sweetened chai served in small glasses, traditionally shared as a "cutting" (half-glass) between two people at street stalls.
  • Kashmiri noon chai (sheer chai): a striking exception to the sweet-spiced formula: a pink, lightly salted tea made with baking soda, green tea leaves and milk, closer in spirit to Central Asian milk teas than to masala chai.
  • Irani chai: found in Irani cafes established by Zoroastrian Persian immigrants, particularly around Hyderabad and Mumbai, typically strong black tea served with condensed milk rather than the standard spice blend.
  • South India: chai exists, but shares daily-ritual status with filter coffee, which holds a comparably central place in states like Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.
