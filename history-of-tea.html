The history of tea

Every tea legend starts with a single, specific year: 2737 BCE. Real history is messier than that, and more interesting: the actual physical proof of tea drinking arrives more than 2,500 years later than the legend claims, and the industry that today ships tea to nearly every country on earth is barely 200 years old. This is that story, from myth to machine.

2737 BCE: the legend

According to Chinese tradition, the mythical emperor and herbalist Shennong was boiling water beneath a tree when a few leaves blew into his pot. Curious, he drank the infusion anyway, and liked it. It's a founding myth, not a historical record, retold for millennia precisely because it makes such a clean origin story: an accident, a moment of curiosity, and a new drink. No physical evidence supports a date this early, and Shennong himself is a legendary rather than historical figure.

141 BCE: the actual evidence

The real trail starts much later, and it starts with an emperor's tomb rather than a legend. In 2016, researchers analysing plant material excavated from the Han Yangling Mausoleum, the burial site of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, confirmed it was tea: they detected theanine, an amino acid found almost exclusively in the tea plant, along with surface crystals matching modern tea leaves. It remains the oldest confirmed physical evidence of tea anywhere in the world, roughly a century older than the earliest written mentions. Whether the emperor was drinking it for pleasure or taking it as medicine isn't clear from the remains alone, but the tea itself was high-grade, buried with him as something worth taking into the afterlife.

8th century CE: tea becomes a discipline

For most of the centuries between, tea in China moved gradually from a regional, sometimes medicinal plant to something increasingly formalised. That process reached a turning point under the Tang dynasty, when the writer Lu Yu produced the Cha Jing ("The Classic of Tea"): the first known dedicated treatise on tea's cultivation, processing and preparation, and the text that effectively created tea as a subject worth writing seriously about. (More on Lu Yu and the historical figures who followed him is covered on Tea specialists.)

9th to 16th century: tea leaves China

Tea's spread beyond China happened slowly, then compounded. It reached Japan via Buddhist monks as early as the 9th century, but took root culturally in the late 12th century through the monk Eisai, who linked it to Zen practice, a connection that eventually produced the formal Japanese tea ceremony. Tea remained essentially unknown in Europe for centuries afterward: it took Portuguese and Dutch maritime traders reaching East Asia in the late 1500s and early 1600s to bring the first shipments west at all, and even then it arrived as a rare, expensive curiosity rather than a daily drink.

1662: tea becomes fashionable in England

Tea's transformation from curiosity to fashion in Britain is usually traced to a single marriage: in 1662, the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza married England's Charles II and brought her own tea-drinking habit into the English court. Aristocratic fashion did the rest, and tea drinking filtered down through English society over the following century, from court curiosity to fashionable habit to, eventually, a national one.

1600s to 1830s: an empire's dependence on China

As British demand for tea grew, the entire supply chain ran through one place: China, the only source of tea anywhere in the world at that point. The British East India Company built an enormous trade around importing it, and by the early 19th century Britain's dependence on Chinese tea, paid for partly through the opium trade into China, had become a serious strategic and financial problem for the Company. Solving that dependence meant finding a way to grow tea somewhere the British already controlled.

1834 to 1839: Assam and the first tea company outside China

The answer had been growing wild the whole time. In the early 1830s, the Scottish adventurer Robert Bruce, and later his brother Charles, identified a native tea plant growing in Assam, in India's northeast: a distinct variety, later classified as Camellia sinensis var. assamica, different from the Chinese plant but genuinely tea. In 1839, the Assam Company was formed to cultivate it commercially, the first tea company established anywhere outside China. It marked the actual beginning of tea as a plantation crop grown at industrial scale, rather than smallholder cultivation refined over centuries.

1848 to 1851: Robert Fortune's smuggled expertise

Growing tea in India solved half the problem; processing it well was the other half, and that knowledge still belonged to China. Between 1848 and 1851, the Scottish botanist Robert Fortune, working for the East India Company, travelled through China's tea-growing regions in disguise and smuggled out tea plants, seeds, and, just as importantly, Chinese processing expertise and workers, into British India. It's a pivotal and ethically fraught episode: industrial espionage, essentially, and one of the more direct acts that let India develop into a tea-growing rival to China within a few decades. (India's tea regions, and the chai culture that eventually grew from them, are covered in more depth on Tea in Asia and Indian chai.)

1930: the machine takes over

For all of this, tea processing was still fundamentally manual work: withering, rolling and drying leaves by hand or with simple equipment, exactly as it had been for centuries. That changed in 1930, when Sir William McKercher brought the first CTC (crush, tear, curl) machine into service at the Amgoorie Tea Estate in Assam. Rather than rolling whole leaves, CTC machines pass leaf through rollers studded with hundreds of small teeth, shredding it into small, dense granules that oxidise and dry far faster than whole leaf, and brew stronger and quicker as a result. It was the moment tea processing stopped being a craft bottleneck and became an industrial one: CTC tea remains the base for most mass-market black tea and milk tea worldwide, chai included, precisely because it can be produced at a volume and speed whole-leaf processing never could.

~1908: the accident that changed how tea is actually consumed

The last piece of industrialisation happened on the consumer end, and it wasn't intentional. Around 1908, New York tea merchant Thomas Sullivan began mailing small silk sample bags of tea to prospective clients, expecting them to remove the leaves and brew them loose. Instead, several clients simply dropped the whole silk bag into hot water, and asked Sullivan to send more the same way. He hadn't invented the tea bag outright (a fabric tea-bag patent by Roberta Lawson and Mary Molaren already existed, several years earlier), but Sullivan's accidental version is the one that stuck, and once cheaper gauze replaced the silk in the 1920s, the tea bag became the vehicle for the single-serving, mass-produced tea that most of the world drinks today.

From ritual to commodity

Laid end to end, the story runs from a mythical accident in a Chinese legend, to real archaeological proof more than two and a half thousand years later, to centuries of careful, manual refinement, to a British colonial project that moved cultivation across a continent, to two industrial-era inventions, one deliberate and one accidental, that turned tea into the fast, mass-produced commodity it is today. What started as a discipline requiring years to master became something anyone could prepare in minutes, without ever changing what's actually in the cup.
