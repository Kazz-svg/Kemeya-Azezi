Why Milk Actually Softens Tea, and What the "Milk First" Argument Is Really About

By Kemeya Azezi

Whether milk goes into the cup before or after the tea is usually framed as a matter of manners or class, a very British argument settled by preference rather than evidence. Underneath that debate, though, sit two separate pieces of real chemistry: why milk softens a strong tea's harshness in the first place, and whether pouring order actually changes anything measurable about the milk itself.

Why tea feels astringent to begin with

The dry, puckering sensation a strong black tea leaves in the mouth isn't a taste, it's a texture, caused by tannins (plant polyphenols) cross-linking with proline-rich proteins naturally present in saliva. That cross-linking forms a precipitate on the surface of the mouth, which the nervous system reads as astringency. It's the same basic mechanism behind a very tannic red wine or an underripe persimmon.

Casein gets there first

Milk interrupts this before it happens in the mouth at all. Casein, milk's main protein, exists in clusters called micelles, and specific casein types, particularly beta-casein and kappa-casein, sit exposed on the micelle's surface where they readily bind the same tannins that would otherwise target salivary proteins. Once a tannin is bound to casein, it's no longer available to cross-link proteins in the mouth, so the resulting tannin-casein complex passes through largely unnoticed instead of registering as astringent. Milk isn't just diluting a strong tea's harsh edge: it's chemically intercepting the specific compound responsible for it, before the tea ever reaches the tongue.

What the milk-first argument is actually about

That casein-tannin mechanism happens regardless of pouring order, once the two liquids are mixed, it works the same either way. The milk-first debate is about something else: milk proteins denature, meaning their structure breaks down, above roughly 75°C. In 2003, Loughborough University's Dr Andrew Stapley, publicised through the Royal Society of Chemistry, argued that pouring hot tea onto milk already sitting in the cup keeps the combined liquid cooler throughout the mixing process than the reverse order, where cold milk added gradually into fully hot tea can briefly sit in direct contact with liquid well above that threshold before everything fully combines. By that account, milk-in-first exposes less of the milk's protein to denaturing heat over the course of pouring.

A real but modest effect, layered on top

The size of this effect is generally described as small, noticeable mainly with full-fat milk in a thin, fast-cooling cup like porcelain, and it doesn't settle the matter on taste grounds by itself: pouring tea first also lets a drinker see the colour and judge how much milk to add, a practical reason some people prefer it regardless of the chemistry. What's worth taking from it isn't a verdict on which pour order is correct, but that two different mechanisms are at work in the same cup: casein binding tannins, which happens no matter the order, and protein denaturation from heat exposure, which pouring order can modestly influence.
