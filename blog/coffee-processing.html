"Washed" Coffee Is Fermented Coffee, It Just Doesn't Advertise It

"Washed" sounds like the mildest thing that can happen to a coffee cherry: strip the fruit, rinse the seed, done. That description skips the actual step doing most of the work. Getting the fruit off a coffee seed isn't a rinse. It's a fermentation, deliberately run, and it's one of three different fermentation environments that give washed, natural and honey processed coffee their very different characters.

The problem fermentation solves

Once a ripe coffee cherry is depulped, the seed underneath is still coated in mucilage: a sticky, sugar-rich layer that clings to the parchment far too tightly to rinse off directly. Scraping or dissolving it away mechanically at scale isn't practical, so processors lean on biology instead, which is where the "washed" name quietly undersells what's happening.

What's actually fermenting in the tank

Depulped beans go into fermentation tanks, submerged in water or sometimes left to ferment dry, for anywhere from roughly 8 to as long as 72 hours depending on climate, altitude and the result the producer wants. During that window, naturally present yeasts and bacteria, working alongside enzymes the bean itself carries, break down the pectin holding the mucilage together: specific pectinases including pectin lyase, polygalacturonase and pectin methylesterase, loosen it until its texture shifts from slick to grainy, the usual signal producers watch for before washing it off. This is genuine microbial fermentation, not a passive soak, which is part of why a washed coffee can pick up fermented or winey notes if the tank runs too long, even though washed is generally sold as the cleanest, most neutral option on the processing spectrum.

Natural: the same process, running inside the fruit

Natural (dry) processing skips depulping entirely and dries the whole intact cherry, fruit and all, for roughly two to four weeks. Fermentation still happens, but slowly and unevenly, working against the seed from inside the intact fruit rather than in an open tank. That slower, fruit-soaked fermentation is the real source of natural coffee's heavier body and stronger, often winey or distinctly fruity character, and also why natural lots carry more risk of off-flavours if drying goes wrong somewhere inside the cherry, out of sight.

Honey: not an ingredient, a quantity

Honey processing names the amount of mucilage left on the bean during drying, not anything added to it: the sticky layer itself was called "miel," Spanish for honey, by Central American producers, particularly in Costa Rica, where the method is generally credited as originating. Lots are informally graded by how much mucilage stays on through drying: white honey leaves around 10 percent, yellow around 25 percent, red around 50 percent, and black honey 75 to 100 percent. More retained mucilage generally means more sugar available to the bean during drying, which is the main reason darker honey grades tend to taste sweeter.

Three processing names that sound like three different actions, washing, drying, coating, turn out to describe three different fermentation environments instead: a tank of water, the inside of an intact fruit, or some fraction of mucilage deliberately left in contact with the seed. What varies is where the fermentation happens and how long it's allowed to run, not whether it happens at all.
