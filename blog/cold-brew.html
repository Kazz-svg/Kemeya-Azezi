Cold Brewing Doesn't Just Slow Extraction Down, It Changes What Comes Out

By Kemeya Azezi

An earlier post on this site covered why hot water matters for brewing tea properly. Cold brewing looks like the opposite approach entirely, steeping for hours instead of minutes, but it isn't simply the same extraction running in slow motion. Temperature doesn't just change how fast compounds leave the leaf. It changes which compounds leave at all, and by how much, relative to each other.

Extraction is selective, not just slow

Different compounds in tea leaf dissolve into water at different rates depending on temperature, and that rate doesn't scale evenly across compounds. Catechins (the astringent, bitter polyphenols) and caffeine both extract considerably more efficiently in hot water than in cold. Given enough time, cold water does still pull them out, but even a multi-hour cold steep doesn't fully catch up to what a few minutes in hot water accomplishes for those specific compounds, while other flavour and aroma compounds are less temperature-sensitive. The practical result is that cold and hot brews of the same leaf aren't the same drink at a different strength; they're different ratios of the same ingredients.

What an actual comparison found, for tea

A study comparing hot and cold brewing methods for green tea found measurably lower caffeine in the cold-brewed version, along with lower levels of EGCG and EGC, the two catechins most responsible for bitterness and astringency, alongside reports of a less bitter, less astringent cup. Notably, the same study found no significant difference in total tea polyphenol content between the two methods. That's the useful nuance: cold brewing isn't extracting less of everything in proportion. It's extracting comparable total polyphenols while pulling disproportionately less caffeine and fewer of the specific catechins that read as harsh, which is the real mechanism behind cold brew's reputation for a smoother, gentler cup.

Coffee tells a messier story

The same general direction holds for coffee, cold brew is consistently reported as less bitter, but the underlying chemistry is less settled than tea's. A peer-reviewed study found cold brew coffee had genuinely lower titratable acidity than hot brew. A separate peer-reviewed study, though, found chlorogenic acid levels (the specific acidic compounds usually blamed for coffee's brightness) were comparable between hot and cold brews at the same ratio, and that cold brew caffeine levels were sometimes higher than hot brew's, depending on grind size and steep time. Put plainly: the popular claim that cold brew coffee is simply "less acidic coffee" oversimplifies research that doesn't yet agree on why cold brew tastes smoother, which is worth stating honestly rather than repeating as settled fact.

Cold brewing's gentler reputation is real, at least for tea, where the evidence is clearer. But the reason isn't that cold water extracts less. It's that cold water extracts differently, leaving a different, more selective mix of compounds in the cup than hot water would from the exact same leaf.
